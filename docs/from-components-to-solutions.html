Industrial Strategy

From Components to Solutions

How industrial value moves from standard products to tailored systems that work in reality.

Industrial markets are entering a phase where the value of standard components is increasingly under pressure.

Globalization, digital platforms and artificial intelligence are accelerating the commoditization of many industrial products. Technical information is becoming more accessible, suppliers more comparable and standard solutions easier to generate.

In this environment, competing only on the component itself becomes increasingly difficult.

What creates value is no longer only the product, but the capability to transform that product into a tailored solution.

For this reason, component manufacturers may progressively evolve toward a more solution-oriented role. Customers will not simply ask for a mechanical part, a motor, a conveyor or a sensor. They will increasingly ask for integration, customization, process understanding and operational reliability.

The competitive advantage will belong to companies capable of combining three elements:

  • deep knowledge of the component
  • understanding of the industrial process
  • ability to adapt solutions to specific customer realities

This transition is particularly important because artificial intelligence naturally tends toward standardization.

AI systems are extremely effective at generating optimized average solutions based on existing data and common engineering logic. But industrial reality is often decided by exceptions, constraints and details that are difficult to generalize.

Factories rarely operate in perfect theoretical conditions. Projects encounter installation tolerances, maintenance limitations, operational habits, environmental conditions and unexpected interactions between systems.

These details may appear secondary during the design phase, but they often become decisive during implementation.

One of the risks of relying blindly on AI-generated engineering is losing direct understanding of how products are actually manufactured, assembled and used in real industrial environments.

A technically convincing solution on paper may fail because of a small practical detail:

  • accessibility for maintenance
  • mechanical tolerances
  • operator behavior
  • installation sequence
  • material variability
  • production constraints
  • transport or assembly limitations

For this reason, engineering knowledge does not disappear with AI. Its role becomes more strategic.

The future engineer may increasingly operate at two opposite levels simultaneously:

  • defining the macro vision of the system
  • controlling the micro details that determine whether the project truly works

The "middle layer", repetitive calculations, standard layouts, routine documentation and conventional optimization, may increasingly be handled by AI systems.

But vision and responsibility remain human.

The engineer of the future may therefore become less focused on producing every technical detail manually and more focused on supervising coherence across the entire system: understanding where automation is reliable, where customization is necessary and where human intuition still matters.

In this scenario, companies capable of combining AI efficiency with deep process knowledge will likely dominate industrial markets.

Because when standard solutions become accessible to everyone, the real value moves toward the ability to understand complexity, adapt intelligently and make systems work in reality, not only in theory.
