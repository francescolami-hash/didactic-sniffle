Automation & AI in Mechanical Engineering

The value of asking the right questions and supervising the result.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly transforming the way engineers work. In mechanical engineering and industrial automation, AI is no longer just a futuristic concept: it is becoming a practical tool that can support design activities, technical sales, documentation, simulation and decision-making.

However, the real competitive advantage does not come from simply "using AI". It comes from understanding how to interact with it correctly.

Today, one of the most important engineering skills is the ability to ask the right questions.

An AI system can generate calculations, technical concepts, presentations, reports, software code and commercial documentation in seconds. Yet the quality of the output strongly depends on the quality of the human guidance behind it.

For this reason, the role of the engineer is evolving.

The engineer is no longer only the person who manually produces every single technical element. Increasingly, the engineer becomes the supervisor of an intelligent workflow: defining objectives, identifying constraints, verifying assumptions and evaluating results.

This changes the nature of technical work.

In industrial automation, for example, AI can support:

  • generation of technical documentation
  • layout and process analysis
  • preliminary mechanical concepts
  • commercial presentations and sales support
  • optimization of industrial flows
  • interpretation of customer requirements
  • software assistance and automation logic
  • rapid access to technical knowledge

But AI still requires human judgment.

An incorrect assumption, an imprecise prompt or a misunderstood constraint can generate technically convincing but wrong results. Because of this, supervision becomes essential.

The future engineer may therefore resemble less a traditional operator and more a conductor coordinating intelligent systems.

This transition is particularly relevant in complex industrial environments where mechanical systems, software, logistics and business decisions interact continuously. The ability to connect technical knowledge with strategic reasoning becomes increasingly valuable.

Another important aspect is speed.

AI allows engineers and technical sales professionals to reduce the time between idea and execution. Tasks that previously required days of research or drafting can now be explored in minutes. This creates more space for higher-level thinking: evaluating alternatives, improving solutions and understanding customer needs.

Yet technology alone is not enough.

Critical thinking, technical intuition and the capacity to recognize meaningful results remain deeply human abilities.

For this reason, the future of engineering is unlikely to be a replacement of people by AI. More realistically, it will be a collaboration between human expertise and intelligent tools.

The professionals who will benefit the most are not necessarily those who know every technical detail manually, but those capable of guiding systems effectively, validating outputs and transforming information into decisions.

In this sense, artificial intelligence does not reduce the importance of engineering culture. It increases its importance.

Because when machines become capable of generating answers, the real difference is made by the people capable of asking the right questions.
